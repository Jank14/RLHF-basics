[4] The loop: the agent acts, the environment answers
Week 1, slide one. The agent is the policy network; the environment is CartPole. Every step: the agent sees the state, picks an action, the environment returns a reward and the next state. Six words describe any RL problem. Keep this loop in mind: in Part 4 we fill it in again, for GPT-2.

[5] The policy is a classifier whose classes are actions [SKIP IF SHORT]
An image classifier maps an image to class probabilities. A policy maps a state to action probabilities: 4 numbers in, 2 probabilities out. To act, we sample. Remember this: in Part 4, a language model is exactly this classifier with 50,257 classes.

[6] One game of CartPole: rewards, the return G, and the "usual"
One short game, slowed down. Top row: the pole tips further each step and falls after 5 steps. Each step the agent pushes and gets +1. Click: the return G_t is the reward still to come from step t: 5 from the start, 4 from step 1, down to 1. Click: but is 5 good or bad? We need something to compare with. VPG's "usual": the average return of the other envs at the same step. Here four envs got 5, 40, 120 and 15 from step 0, average 45. Click: score = G − usual = 5 − 45 = −40: the first push was worse than usual, so make it less likely. Positive score → more likely.

[7] VPG: climb the average return by gradient ascent
The whole of VPG on one slide. The goal: J of theta, the average return of the games our policy plays; we want it big. Click: play 32 games with the current policy. Click: for every step, the usual b_t is simply the average of G_t over the 32 games at that step; score = G_t minus b_t. Click: the gradient of J is approximated by the average of grad log pi of the action taken times its score: good actions get their log-probability pushed up, bad ones down. We take one step uphill: theta plus learning rate times that. Click: with the new weights we play brand-new games and throw the old ones away, and repeat: 40 rounds in last week's notebook. The average return climbs.

[8–10] VPG has three problems; PPO fixes each one
Three problems, from the 4 pm session. One: the usual is the same number for every game at a step, whatever state that game is in: an upright pole and a falling pole at step 100 get the same baseline. Fix: a critic network that knows what is usual for each state. Two: it is wasteful: 16,000 steps of play buy one gradient step and are thrown away. Fix: reuse each batch for several steps, corrected with importance weights. Three: but if we take many steps on one batch, the policy can move too far from the one that played it. Fix: the clip. One slide for each.

[11] Fix 1: a critic network learns what is usual for each state
VPG's weak spot one: the "usual" was the same number for every env at a step, whatever state it was in. An upright pole and a falling pole at step 100 got the same baseline. PPO adds a second network, the critic. Left: it takes the state, 4 numbers, and outputs one number V(s): the return we usually get from this state. Click: so the upright state gets about 400, the falling one about 5. Click: how it learns: after each batch we know the returns actually achieved, so we train V(s) to match them: value loss = (V(s) − G)², squared error, nothing new. Click: how it is used: score A = G − V(s). The falling pole that got saved and earned 150 scores +145, clearly good. VPG's step average of 200 would have called it −50, bad. Today the critic is a small head on GPT-2: Part 5.

[12] Fix 2: reuse each batch, corrected by importance weights
Weak spot two: VPG plays 16,000 steps, takes one gradient step, and throws the data away. PPO wants several steps per batch. Problem: after the first step the policy has changed. The batch was played by the old policy, so it is no longer a sample from the current one. Fix: re-weight every action by how much more or less likely the current policy finds it: the ratio r = π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s). Example: when played, "push right" had probability 0.40; after two updates it has 0.50; r = 1.25, so this action now counts 1.25 times. The objective becomes r × A instead of log π × A. This ratio is the importance weight: re-weighting the old policy's games by it estimates how the new policy would score on them, without playing again. The notebook computes it per token from stored log-probs: r = exp(logprob_new − logprob_old).

[13] Fix 3: clip, so one batch can't move the policy too far
Why the clip is needed. The batch only tells us about actions near what π_old did. If we keep stepping on the same batch, a good action's probability keeps rising: r goes 1.1, 1.3, 1.8, 3. The objective r × A keeps rewarding that, but the data says nothing about a policy that far from the one that played; we overshoot and the policy can collapse. The clip: once r leaves the band 0.8 to 1.2 in the direction A wants, the objective goes flat, gradient zero, stop pushing. Picture: for a good action (A > 0) the gain rises with r up to 1.2, then flat. For a bad action the same on the left at 0.8. So each action moves at most about 20% per batch. In the notebook calc_clipped_surrogate_objective is given, same as last week.

[14] PPO: collect a batch, then maximise three terms
PPO runs in phases. Rollout: play with the current policy and store states, actions, log-probs, advantages. Learning: several minibatch steps on one objective with three terms: the clipped term pushes better-than-usual actions up; minus the critic's error so the value estimates improve; plus a small entropy bonus to keep exploring. Today the notebook reuses all three, and adds a fourth term in Part 6.

Today: the agent is GPT-2, its action is the next token, and its reward is a score for the text it wrote. The learning algorithm is still PPO, with one extra term. [20] Pretraining: predict the next word, with labels for free
A language model is the classifier from Part 1 with 50,257 classes, one per token. Pretraining: take internet text; at every position the label is simply the next token in the text. The loss is minus the log of the probability the model gave to the real next token: cross-entropy, as for any classifier. If the model gave " mat" 40%, the loss is −log 0.4 ≈ 0.92; if it had given 90%, 0.11. Labels cost nothing and there are trillions of them, which is why pretraining scales.

[21–23] A pretrained model is not an assistant: it continues text
SCRIPT. "Pretraining taught the model one thing: what text usually comes next on the internet. That is not the same as answering you. Here are three published examples from GPT-3, a much bigger cousin of our GPT-2. Left, from the InstructGPT paper: asked to write a short story about a time-travelling frog, GPT-3 doesn't write the story. It writes another writing prompt, because on the internet a line like that usually sits in a list of assignments. Middle, from TruthfulQA: asked what happens if you smash a mirror, it says seven years of bad luck. A superstition is common text, so it is a likely continuation, even though it is false. Right, InstructGPT again: given a nonsense question about eating socks after meditating, it plays along. Nothing is broken here: the model does exactly what it was trained to do. GPT-2 does the same, just less fluently. But people now ask these models for help, for facts and for advice, so we need answers that follow the request, are true, and are safe. Pretraining alone does not give us that." Note: the frog prompt was in French in the paper; translated here.

[24–25] Make it an assistant: show it examples of helpful answers
How the field responded. If a pretrained model copies the kind of text it saw, then show it the kind of text we want: an instruction followed by a good response. This is instruction tuning, also called supervised fine-tuning, SFT. FLAN (Google, 2021) fine-tuned on many tasks phrased as instructions and the model got much better at following new instructions. InstructGPT (OpenAI, 2022) used demonstrations written by people as its first step, before RLHF. Click: same prompt as before: after instruction tuning the model writes the story instead of another assignment. Click: and this is still step 1 of every chat model today. Next slide: exactly how it is done.

[26–27] SFT, also called instruction tuning: train on written answers
The first fix, used by every chat model. Click: the data are instruction–response pairs written by people, put into a fixed text format. Click: training uses exactly the pretraining loss, minus log probability of each next token, but only on the response tokens; the instruction tokens are masked out because we don't want the model to learn to write instructions. Click: InstructGPT used about 13,000 prompts with answers written by labellers. When the data are instruction→response pairs like this, SFT is what people call instruction tuning (FLAN used the same idea across many tasks). Click: result: the model answers in an assistant format instead of continuing a document.

[28] SFT is expensive: every training answer is written by a person
SFT works, but look at where the data comes from. Every example needs a person to write the ideal answer: minutes per answer, and for code, medicine or law it needs an expert. You need thousands, covering every kind of request: InstructGPT used about 13,000 prompts and a team of about 40 contractors. Click: and even with all that, two limits remain. The model can only become as good as the people writing, since it copies them. And it only ever sees good human answers, never its own mistakes, so it gets no signal about which of its own answers are bad. Click: is there a cheaper signal that fixes both? Next slide.

[29–31] Judging answers is cheaper, and fixes both limits
The cheaper signal. Instead of a person writing the ideal answer, let the model write a few answers itself and have a person say which is better. Judging takes seconds, almost anyone can do it, even where writing is hard: few of us can write a great poem or proof, most of us can pick the better of two. And because the answers are the model's own, the judgments tell it which of its own behaviours to do more of and which to do less of. Click: but RL needs a number, not "A beats B". Turning judgments into a number is the reward model: Part 3.

[32] A model is fine-tuned only on human-written ideal answers. What does that data NOT tell it?
Run the poll on Slido (concept question, no calculation). Answer: B — Which of its own likely answers are worse than others. Why: Demonstrations only say "write this". They never compare two answers or mark the model's own mistakes; a ranking or a reward does.

[34] Part: From human feedback to a reward
The reward model in real RLHF, then today's notebook reward.

[35] RL needs a number: a reward model learns our judgments
The core idea of RLHF. We want the model to do what people prefer. RL can do that, but RL needs a number for every answer the model tries, and during training it tries an enormous number of answers, far too many for people to rate as they happen. So we split the job. First, people judge a manageable set of answers. Then we train a model, the reward model, to predict those judgments: it reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number, high if people would like it. From then on, that model stands in for the people and scores every answer RL produces, instantly. Click through the chain.

[36–38] The original RLHF pipeline: three steps
This is how OpenAI trained InstructGPT in 2022, the recipe behind ChatGPT. Read each column top to bottom, then its data box. Step 1, SFT: people wrote good answers to prompts, about 13,000 of them, and the model was fine-tuned on those answers. Step 2, the reward model: for each of about 33,000 prompts, the SFT model was asked the SAME prompt several times. Because we sample tokens, each run gives a different answer; InstructGPT collected between 4 and 9 answers per prompt. One labeller then put that set of answers in order, best to worst. A scorer R is trained to agree with those orderings. Why several answers at once? Ranking K answers gives K times (K minus 1) over 2 comparisons for the price of reading K answers: 4 answers give 6 pairs, 9 give 36. Step 3, RL: about 31,000 prompts and no people at all: the model answers, R scores the answer, and PPO makes high-scoring answers more likely. Next: step 2 in detail.

[39–40] Building the comparison data: the model answers, a person ranks
Step 2a in detail, to clear up "4 to 9 replies". Take ONE prompt. Ask the SFT model that same prompt several times. Because every token is sampled, each run gives a different answer. InstructGPT collected between 4 and 9 answers per prompt; here 4. One labeller reads all of them and puts them in order. Here: A is a good child-friendly answer, B is correct but too technical, C ignores the request, D is false. Click: a ranking of 4 answers contains 6 comparisons, each saying "this one beat that one": K answers give K times K minus 1 over 2 pairs, so 9 answers give 36. These pairs are the training data. InstructGPT did this for about 33,000 prompts. Nobody had to write an answer: the person only judged. (Answers here are illustrative.)

[41–44] Reward model objective: good replies score above bad
The objective in one sentence: we want the reward model to give the reply people preferred a higher score than the reply they rejected. For one pair, take the score difference R(winner) − R(loser) and squash it with a sigmoid: that is the model's probability that the winner really is better. The loss is minus the log of that probability, so it is small when the model agrees with the person and large when it disagrees. Worked example, right order: scores 1.2 and 0.4, difference 0.8, sigmoid 0.69, loss = −log(0.69) = 0.37, small. Wrong order: 0.2 and 0.9, difference −0.7, sigmoid 0.33, loss = −log(0.33) = 1.10, large; the gradient pushes A up and B down. The training objective is the average of this loss over all pairs: minimise it, which is the same as maximising the probability that the reward model agrees with the people.

[45–49] How "A > B" becomes a higher score for A
One training step of the reward model, one piece per click. Top row: the comparison data, collected once before training: a prompt, the SFT model answers it twice (InstructGPT used 4 to 9; 2 here for clarity), a person says A is better than B. Bottom row: the training step. Click: the reward model reads prompt plus reply and outputs one number for each: right now it scores A 0.2 and B 0.9, the wrong way round. Click: the loss from the last slide, minus log sigma of the score gap: 1.10, large because the model disagrees with the person. Click: the key question, how does the label A > B turn into a higher score for A? The loss depends only on the gap R(A) − R(B). Its gradient with respect to R(A) is −(1 − σ) = −0.67, so gradient descent RAISES R(A); with respect to R(B) it is +0.67, so it LOWERS R(B). Backpropagation carries that into the reward model's weights, so replies like A score higher from now on. Click: after one step, illustrative: R(A) 0.4, R(B) 0.7, loss 0.85. Repeat over every pair in the dataset and the scores end up following people's rankings. Then R is frozen and used in step 3.

[50] Step 3: write, score, update (the notebook's picture)
The notebook's RLHF picture, built one piece at a time. Click 1: a prompt, x, "A dog is…". Both models start as the same SFT model from step 1: we make two copies, freeze one, and train the other. Click 2: the tuned model, the copy we train, writes an answer y: "man's best friend". Click 3: the reward model reads it and gives one number, r. Click 4: PPO uses that reward to update the tuned model's weights, and the loop repeats with the new weights. Click 5: one more piece: a frozen copy of the initial model answers the same prompt. [55] One reward per completion, after the 30th token, not per token
Be precise about when GPT-2 gets rewarded. It writes the completion one token at a time: 30 steps. During writing, no token gets a reward: shown as a dash (in the code it is stored as 0, which just means "nothing yet"). Only when the completion is finished does the reward function read the whole text and return one number: here 2 periods. So the reward is per completion, not per token. Click: last week every step paid +1. Click: so the return from any step, the reward still to come, is the same 2 for every token, and nothing tells us which tokens earned it. Hold that question: it is Part 5.

[56–60] Why the 128 rewards are rescaled before use
Look at the rewards of one phase: 8 of the 128, illustrative values. Top row, phase 1: counts between 0 and 3, mean 1.5, spread (standard deviation) 0.9. Click: rescaled with (r − mean) / std: they sit around 0, spread 1. Click: bottom row, phase 30 of the kl_coef = 0 run, where the notebook's mean reward was about 20: counts from 14 to 27, mean 20, spread 3.9. Click: rescaled, they look exactly like the phase-1 row: mean 0, spread 1. That is the point. Click: why it matters for the gradient. The update is the advantage times the gradient of the log-probability, and advantages are about as big as the spread of the rewards. Without rescaling, the spread grows from 0.9 to 3.9 during training, so at the same learning rate the steps would get about 4 times bigger exactly when the policy is already changing fast. With rescaling the spread is always 1, so the step size stays the same all run, and "better than this batch's average" is always positive. This is not the critic: it only fixes the scale; the critic then learns to predict these rescaled rewards.

[65] GPT-2 as an agent: read the state, pick a token, append it
The notebook's picture, one piece at a time. Click 1, the state s_t: the whole text so far, "The cat sat on the", not just the last word. Click 2 and 3: the whole network, transformer plus unembedding, turns the text into logits; the ones at the LAST position, after softmax, are the probabilities for the next token. The policy π is the whole network: text in, a probability for every token out. (The hidden-state row matters later: the critic reads it.) Click 4: we sample the action a_t, here "mat". Click 5: the transition: the new token is appended to the prompt plus everything written so far, giving s_t+1. Nothing random happens in the environment; all randomness is in the sampling. Same job as last week's policy network: state in, action probabilities out.

[66–70] Choosing the action: keep the top 10 tokens, then sample one
How one action is chosen, with the notebook's settings, step by step; the numbers on the right match the clicks. Step 1: after "This is a", GPT-2 gives a probability to every one of its 50,257 tokens; here are the 12 largest. Step 2: top_k = 10 keeps only the 10 most likely and drops the rest, so rare junk like " zebra" is never picked. Step 3: the 10 kept probabilities are rescaled so they add up to 1. Step 4: lay them end to end on a line from 0 to 1: " new" takes 0 to 0.20, " great" 0.20 to 0.37, " list" 0.37 to 0.50, and so on. Each token's piece is exactly as long as its probability. Step 5: draw one random number u between 0 and 1, uniformly; the computer does this with a random number generator. Say u comes out 0.42: it lands in " list"'s piece, so the action is " list". Because " list" covers 13% of the line, a random u lands there 13% of the time: each token is chosen exactly as often as its probability. That is all "sampling" means. Temperature 1.0 means the probabilities are used as they are, and the prompt is copied 128 times, so 128 different completions come out. Why sample rather than always take the top token? Exploration, as in week 1: RL only learns from what it tries.

[71] Our PPO is last week's PPO, with three changes
What stays and what changes, piece by piece. The actor: a small policy network then, GPT-2 now. Reuse of each batch with importance weights and the clip: unchanged. The critic: last week a separate network; today a small head on GPT-2, Part 5. The score: last week GAE, which blends many one-step scores; today just the one-step score r_t + V(s_t+1) − V(s_t), where r_t is 0 for every token except the last; also Part 5. The objective: last week clipped term minus value loss plus entropy; today one more term, minus kl_coef times the KL penalty, Part 6. And the unit: one action used to be a push; now each token is one action, with its own ratio.

[76] Part: Which tokens earned the reward?
Credit assignment with a value head.

[77] One reward for the whole completion: which tokens earned it?
One running example for this whole part. The completion " a list. Not much. Done." after "This is" gets reward 3: three periods. PPO needs a score, an advantage, for each of the 8 tokens. Click: score each token by its return G_t, the reward still to come: every token has G_t = 3. Then " a", " Not" and " Done" get exactly as much credit as the three periods that actually earned the reward. It works on average, but it is very noisy. Click: we want the periods to get the credit. To do that, we need something that can tell, while the text is being written, how many periods we are now on track for. That is a critic.

[78–82] The critic forecasts the final reward from the text so far
Recap from 4 pm, left: the critic read the CartPole state and output V(s), the return usually obtained from there. The advantage was what actually happened minus that usual value. Right, today: V(s_t) is the critic's forecast of the final reward, here the final number of periods, given only the text written so far. The bars make that one number visible as two parts. Amber: periods already written: certain, you can count them in the text. Teal: periods still expected: that part is the critic's guess. The bar's total is the one number the critic outputs. "This is a list": nothing written yet, it expects about 0.7 from experience. "This is a list.": one period banked plus 0.5 expected, 1.5. "…Not much.": two banked plus 0.2, 2.2. The teal part is learned from thousands of completions and their final counts, not from rules. Click: what the critic does inside the advantage: it subtracts what was expected anyway. If a token does not change the forecast, its credit is zero, however good the final reward was. A token only gets credit for changing the forecast. That is the next three slides.

[83] Same picture, one new arrow: the value head
Back to the notebook's picture from Part 4. Everything is the same: text in, hidden states, logits, the sampled action. Click: one new arrow. From the hidden state at each position, a small network, the value head, outputs one number V(s_t). Because the hidden states already summarise the whole text so far, a small network on top is enough. Do we only use the last token? For V(s_t) we read the hidden state at the last token of s_t. Because attention only looks backwards, the hidden state at position t has seen exactly the tokens up to t, so running the full completion once gives V for every prefix at the same time: 33 positions, 33 forecasts. One forward pass now gives two outputs: the logits, the actor, and V, the critic. At 4 pm the critic was a separate network; here actor and critic share GPT-2's body.

[84] The value head: a small network, 768 → 3072 → 1
The value head as the notebook builds it. Input: the 768-number hidden state at one position. Which hidden state exactly? The one after GPT-2's final layernorm, ln_final.hook_normalized: the very vector that W_U turns into logits. Layernorm rescales every hidden vector to a standard size, so the head always sees inputs on the same scale. Then a wider hidden layer of 3072 neurons with ReLU, then one output neuron: V. Click by click. 33 forecasts: a completion has 33 positions, 3 prompt tokens plus 30 written, and the head runs at every position, one forecast per prefix. Two learning rates: the optimiser has two parameter groups; the head starts random and learns fast, 5e-4; GPT-2 is already trained and learns gently, 2e-5. vf_coef: the critic loss enters the total objective multiplied by vf_coef = 0.15: it is the weight of the critic loss, the same idea as kl_coef later. Because the body is shared and nothing is detached, the gradient of 0.15 × critic loss flows through the head into GPT-2's own weights too, so GPT-2 learns from the policy objective and, a little, from the critic loss.

[85–88] After each token: how many periods will this text end with?
What this slide is for: we want to see WHERE the critic's forecast moves while the text is written, because on the next slide those moves become each token's credit. The question the critic answers after every token: how many periods will this completion have when it is finished? Its answer is periods already written, amber, plus periods still expected, teal. The green part alone is NOT the value: the whole bar, amber plus green, is V(s_t), the one number the critic outputs. Why is it 0.6 and not a whole number? Any single completion ends with a whole number of periods, but from "This is" many endings are still possible. V is the average over them: if half of such completions end with 0 periods, 40% with 1 and 10% with 2, the average is 0×0.5 + 1×0.4 + 2×0.1 = 0.6. An expected value, so a real number. Click through the tokens. After "This is": nothing written, about 0.6 expected: an average completion. " a": no change. " list": expected 0.7, lists tend to have short items. Now the period. Why does V jump by about 1? Not because of some probability of "seeing" a reward: the reward at the end counts EVERY period in the final text, including this one. Why certain? GPT-2 only ever appends tokens; it never goes back and edits. So a period that has been written will be in the final text, and reward_fn will count it: amber goes up by exactly 1. The guess for the rest drops a little, 0.7 to 0.5, because there is less text left. Total 1.5. " Not", " much": nothing banked, the guess wobbles. Second period: banked again, 2.2. " Done": no change. Final period: the episode ends and we get the real reward, 3. Illustrative numbers in raw periods; the notebook's critic predicts rescaled rewards, same idea.

[89–92] What exactly does the critic predict after each token?
A question students always ask: is the critic predicting how many periods we'll get from now on? No. It predicts the TOTAL number of periods the finished text will have, and that total includes the periods already written. Why the total? Because that is what the reward is: reward_fn counts every period in the finished text, once, at the end. Click: the first period, split into its two parts. Before it, after "This is a list": 0 periods written, about 0.7 expected later: V = 0.7. After it, "This is a list.": 1 period written, about 0.5 expected later: V = 1.5. Click: three points. One: the period is now part of the total, for sure, because GPT-2 only appends text and never edits it, so this period will be counted at the end. Two: the period does NOT make other periods more likely. Look at the expected part: it went DOWN, 0.7 to 0.5, because there is less text left to write. Three: the total still jumps from 0.7 to 1.5, because the written part went up by 1. That jump, +0.8, is exactly the period's credit on the next slide.

[93–97] Credit = how much the token moved the forecast
The notebook's formula first, with every symbol: A_t = r_t + V(s_t+1) − V(s_t). r_t is the reward received at step t. V(s_t+1) is the forecast after the token is written; V(s_t) the forecast before. Click: for every token except the last, nothing has been paid yet: r_t = 0, so the formula becomes V(after) − V(before): did writing this token raise or lower the expected final count? Click: for the last token, r_t is the real reward r, and there is no "after": the episode is over and nothing more will come, so V(after) is 0. The credit is r − V(before). Click: the numbers, same running example. Click: the three periods get +0.8 each, " list" +0.1, " Not" −0.1, the rest 0. Exactly the credit we wanted. The credits add up to r minus the very first forecast, 3 − 0.6 = 2.4: the total surprise, shared out where the forecast moved. In code: the "after" row is values shifted one position left with the reward put in the last slot; the "before" row is values; subtract.

[98] How the critic learns: its target is the next forecast
Where do the forecasts come from? The critic is trained in the same phases, with the same formula. Its target at step t is r_t + V(s_t+1): the reward received now plus the forecast one step later. Before the last token r_t is 0, so the target is just the next forecast. At the last token the target is r, the real reward: here r = 3 because reward_fn counted 3 periods in this finished completion. Learning a guess from the next guess is called temporal-difference learning, TD; it is what the notebook's value loss does. Read a column: after " list" the critic said 0.7, but one step later, after ".", it said 1.5, so the target for " list" is 1.5 and the squared error is 0.64. Notice each error is exactly the credit from the last slide: the critic is pushed to predict the jump one step earlier next time. Over many completions, the real rewards at the end flow backwards through these targets and the forecasts become accurate. The loss is the mean squared error, the same value loss as last week. In code: returns = advantages + values, which equals exactly these targets.

[108] Fix: let the model change, but not too far from the original GPT-2
What should fix this? Look at the next-token probabilities after "This is a". Left: the original GPT-2. Middle: after the period-spam run, almost everything goes on "......", a token GPT-2 would essentially never write. Right: what we actually want: still recognisably GPT-2, just leaning a little towards period-friendly words. So the rule we want: the policy may change, but not a lot, compared with the original. To enforce that we need two things: a frozen copy of the original GPT-2 to compare against, π_base, and a single number for how far the trained model's distribution π_PPO has moved from it. That number is the KL divergence.

[109] KL divergence: one number for "how far has the policy moved?"
One-line intuition first: KL measures how surprised the original GPT-2 would be, on average, by what the new model writes. 0 means no surprise: the two models predict the same. Then the formula: sum over every possible next token x of π_PPO(x) times log of π_PPO(x) over π_base(x). Click by click: x is one candidate next token; the sum runs over all 50,257. π_PPO(x) is the probability the model we are training gives x. π_base(x) is the probability the frozen original gives x. Their ratio, inside the log: log is 0 if unchanged, positive if x became more likely, negative if less likely. Each log is weighted by π_PPO(x), how often the trained model actually writes x. The total is 0 only when the two distributions are identical, and positive otherwise.

[110] Worked example: almost all the KL comes from "......"
The formula on real-looking numbers at one position, after "This is a". Columns: the original's probability, the trained model's, their ratio, its log, and the weighted term. " list" and " story" became a bit less likely, ratio 0.75: log −0.29, weighted −0.09 and −0.04. Negative terms are fine: they appear whenever a token became less likely. "......" went from 0.1% to 20%: 200 times more likely, log +5.3, weighted +1.06. That one row is the whole story. "All others" moved a little. Add the last column: about 0.88, positive as always. The notebook computes this at all 30 generated positions of all 128 completions, takes the mean, multiplies by kl_coef = 2.5, and subtracts it from the objective: next slide.

[111] The fourth term: subtract kl_coef × KL
Remember the "+ ?" box on last week's PPO objective: this is it. The objective becomes clipped term, minus critic loss, plus entropy, minus kl_coef times the KL. Click: kl_coef. It is a hyperparameter: a number we choose before training, not something the model learns. It is the weight on the KL term, the same idea as vf_coef on the critic loss. The notebook uses 2.5. Bigger kl_coef means staying close to GPT-2 matters more compared with getting reward. Click: what it does to the model's choices at one position. Junk: put 20% on "......": KL 0.88, penalty 2.5 × 0.88 = 2.2. Normal English: lean a bit more on " list", which often starts a short sentence that ends in a period: KL 0.05, penalty 0.11. Both routes can earn periods; the junk route costs twenty times more.

[112–115] Why last week's clip doesn't do the KL's job
A fair question: at the very start, π_old IS π_base, the original GPT-2. So in phase 1 the clip and the KL measure from the same model. Why isn't the clip enough? Because π_old does not stay there. The clip compares the new policy with π_old, the policy that wrote THIS batch, and π_old is replaced at every phase. So the clip only says: don't move more than about ×1.2 from where you are NOW. Follow "......", which starts at 0.1%. Phase 1: at most ×1.2 from 0.1%: 0.12%. Phase 2: π_old is now the phase-1 policy, so another ×1.2: 0.14%. Every step is clipped, yet ×1.2 compounds: 0.6% at phase 10, 3.8% at phase 20, 24% at phase 30. That is how the period-spam run escaped with every individual step clipped. The clip also never pulls back: past ×1.2 it just stops pushing. The KL compares with π_base, which never moves, so the further the policy drifts the harder it pushes back: the teal curve levels off (illustrative). Clip = small steps from where you are. KL = stay near the original.

[117] Two ways the trained model can move away from GPT-2
Before the next result we need one more property of the KL. The trained model can drift from GPT-2 in two different ways. Left: the original's next-token probabilities after "This is a". Click, inventing: the model starts writing something GPT-2 almost never writes: "......" goes from 0.1% to 20%, the other tokens shrink to make room. Click, dropping: the model stops writing something GPT-2 did write: " story" goes from 20% to 0.1%, and its probability moves to the other tokens. Both are real changes to the distribution. Click: which one should a penalty for "drifting from GPT-2" charge more for? Ask the room before the next slide. Why we care: a chat model should be allowed to stop saying bad things the base model sometimes says, but should not start writing things the base model never would.

[118–119] The KL charges for inventing, much less for dropping
The answer, computed with the formula on the three cases. Dropping " story" from 20% to 0.1%: KL 0.22. Inventing "......" at 20%: 0.88, four times more. Full period spam, "......" at 85%: 5.45, twenty-five times more. Why the difference? Look at the formula: the KL is an average of log(new / original) weighted by π_PPO, by how often the NEW model writes each token. Click. Dropping: the new model hardly writes " story" any more, so " story" barely enters the average. The only cost is that its 20% moved onto tokens GPT-2 already likes, which are each only a little more likely than before. In fact dropping a token completely can never cost more than log(1/(1 − 0.2)) = 0.22. Inventing: the new model writes "......" often, and every time it does, GPT-2 finds it 200 times less likely, log 200 = 5.3. Heavy weight times big log: expensive, and unbounded as the token gets rarer. That is exactly the behaviour we want: stopping a behaviour is cheap, writing what GPT-2 never would is expensive. This is why the notebook uses KL(π_PPO ‖ π_base) and not the reverse.

[129] Always writing one text costs exactly how surprising it is
Here is the precise reason. Suppose the model collapses completely and always writes one text s. Then π_PPO puts probability 1 on s. Put that into the KL formula: one term, 1 times log of 1 over P_base(s): the KL is exactly minus the log-probability of s under GPT-2: how surprising s is to GPT-2. No approximation. So collapsing costs: onto the rush transcript, about 6 to 19; onto one ordinary sentence, about 63 to 72; onto period spam, 131 to 168. For the rush transcript that is 4 to 10 times cheaper than committing to an ordinary sentence, and it brings 2 to 4 periods. Among all texts the model could commit to, memorised boilerplate full of periods is the bargain. That is the loophole: the KL protects against strange text, not against repeating very familiar text.

[130] Why it snowballs: a feedback loop
Why does it take over instead of staying one sample in a hundred? A feedback loop. One: by chance a few of the 128 samples drift toward the transcript opening. Two: they score well: several periods and almost no KL. Three: so their advantages are positive, and PPO makes those tokens more likely. Four: next phase, more of the 128 samples look like it, and the loop goes round again. Each round makes the favourite more likely, and the batch average rises, so ordinary samples now look below average and get pushed down. Nothing in the objective rewards variety: the entropy bonus exists, but with ent_coef = 0.001 it is far too weak to stop this. By phase 98, two of the three printed samples are the identical text. That is mode collapse: the model narrows onto one high-scoring answer.

The trainer, PEFT and LoRA, then GRPO.

[140–145] Rollout: write 128 completions, then record what we need
One phase has two halves: collect, then learn. This is collect. Step 1: copy the prompt "This is" 128 times; GPT-2 writes 30 tokens for each: 128 completions. Step 2: score them: reward_fn counts periods, then the rewards are rescaled. Step 3: run the model we train over the finished completions once, just to record numbers, not to learn yet: for each written token, the log-probability it had (that is π_old, needed later for the importance weight), and the critic's forecast after each token. Step 4: run the frozen original over them too and record its predictions, needed for the KL. Step 5: compute each token's credit. Learning has not happened yet; "no gradients" just means we are only recording. Everything recorded is stored for the learning half: next slide.

[146–148] Learning: use the same 128 completions for 8 small steps
The learn half. We have 128 completions in memory. Yes, it is just minibatches: shuffle the 128 and cut them into 4 groups of 32. Take one gradient step per group: 4 steps, and every completion has been used once. Then shuffle again into 4 NEW groups of 32 from the same 128 and take 4 more steps: every completion has now been used twice, 8 steps in total. The notebook calls this batches_per_learning_phase = 2 and num_minibatches = 4. Why each piece, on the right. Groups of 32: four smaller updates per pass instead of one big one, and 32 completions fit comfortably in GPU memory. Using each completion twice: writing 128 completions is the slow part, so we squeeze more learning out of each rollout; the importance weights and the clip are exactly what make reusing old samples safe. Shuffling: each step sees a different random mix, so the same completions are not always updated together, the same reason you shuffle a dataset every epoch. Each step: run the current model on its 32 completions, compute the objective (clipped term, critic loss, entropy, KL), backpropagate, one Adam step. After 8 steps: step the learning-rate schedule, throw the memory away, and start a new rollout with the updated model. 100 phases.

[154–158] Full fine-tuning: the memory each trained weight needs
Why memory is the problem. In section 1 every weight of GPT-2 is trained, and every trained weight needs more than itself in memory. One: the weight, the number the model computes with. Two: its gradient, computed by backprop each step: which way, and how much, to change it. Three and four: Adam's two running averages, which is what you asked about. Adam keeps, for every trained weight, m, a running average of its recent gradients: the direction it has been moving lately, which smooths out noisy steps, like momentum. And v, a running average of its squared gradients: how large its gradients usually are. Adam's step divides m by the square root of v, so a weight with big, steady gradients and a weight with small, noisy ones both take sensibly sized steps. These are 2 extra numbers per trained weight: memory the optimiser keeps, not parameters of the model; they are never used to compute outputs. Five, RLHF only: the frozen π_base copy for the KL. That is 5 numbers per weight; at 4 bytes each, GPT-2 small's 124 million weights need about 2.5 GB, an 8-billion-parameter model about 160 GB, before counting activations. (The notebook's optimiser uses maximize=True, so the step is +.)

[159–161] PEFT: freeze almost everything, train a small extra set
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, PEFT: freeze almost every weight and train only a small extra set. A frozen weight needs only itself: no gradient, no Adam averages. And because it never changes, the frozen weights can double as the reference model. Left, full fine-tuning: 124 million weights times 5 numbers, about 2.5 GB. Right, section 2: 124 million frozen weights at 1 number each, plus about 4.3 million trainable numbers, the LoRA adapters and the value head, at 4 numbers each: about 0.6 GB, four times less, and the saving grows with model size because the trainable part stays tiny. Click: ways to choose the small set: freeze most layers and train a few, add adapters, learn soft prompts, or LoRA, which the notebook uses. Click: how this relates to RLHF. They answer different questions. RLHF is WHAT we optimise: reward minus KL, with PPO. PEFT is WHICH weights we let change. Section 2 is exactly section 1's RLHF, but only LoRA's matrices and the value head learn.

[162–163] Fine-tuning = learning an edit to each weight matrix
Before any LoRA notation, one idea. Every weight matrix W in GPT-2, for example the query matrix of one layer, is a big table of numbers. Fine-tuning, whatever method, ends with a new table: W_new = W + ΔW, where ΔW, "delta W", is the edit: how much each number changed. Left: full fine-tuning learns that edit directly, every single number of it, the same size as W. Click: right, LoRA's bet. The edits fine-tuning actually makes are simple: they change the layer in only a few ways, not in 589,824 independent ways. The notebook says it: in practice the change is low-rank. So LoRA builds the edit from two thin pieces, A and B, and only learns those. W itself is never touched. Click: what does "an edit built from a few pieces" mean? Next slide: a tiny example with 4 numbers you can do in your head.

[164] LoRA in miniature: 4 inputs, 4 outputs, rank 1
Shrink everything: a layer with 4 inputs and 4 outputs, and rank r = 1. Click: the input for one token, x = (1, 0, 2, 1). Click: A is a single column of 4 numbers, (1, 0, 0, 1). Multiplying x by A gives ONE number: 1×1 + 0×0 + 2×0 + 1×1 = 2. Think of A as a detector: it measures one thing about the input, here "input 1 plus input 4", and reports a single reading, z = 2. Click: B is a single row of 4 numbers, (0.5, 0, −0.5, 0). Multiply the reading by B: 2 × (0.5, 0, −0.5, 0) = (1, 0, −1, 0). Think of B as a nudge: which way to push the output, and the reading says how hard. That vector is the correction added to the layer's normal output x W. Click: the edit ΔW = A·B as a full table: every row is a copy of B, just scaled: rows 1 and 4 are B, rows 2 and 3 are zero. Whatever the input, the correction always points along B: that is what rank 1 means. Click: GPT-2 with the notebook's r = 4: A has 4 columns, so 4 detectors, giving 4 readings for each token: those are the "4 numbers" in the middle of the adapter. B has 4 rows: 4 nudge directions. Training learns what each detector looks for and which way each one nudges. Full edit for this toy: 16 numbers; A plus B: 8. For a 768 by 768 matrix: 589,824 versus 6,144.

[165–168] r readings: A has r columns, B has r rows
On the last slide r was 1: A was ONE column, so x times A gave ONE number, and B was ONE row, so the correction could only point one way. Where do "4 directions" come from? From more columns. Click: rank 2, same input x = (1, 0, 2, 1). Now A has two columns. Each column is its own detector: x times column 1 = 1 + 1 = 2; x times column 2 = 0 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 1. So x A is now TWO numbers, (2, 1): one reading per column. Click: B now has two rows, each a direction in the output: row 1 is (0.5, 0, −0.5, 0), row 2 is (0, 1, 0, 0). The correction is a mix of the rows, weighted by the readings: 2 times row 1 plus 1 times row 2 = (1, 1, −1, 0). Click: GPT-2 with r = 4. A is 768 by 4: four columns, so four detectors, four readings for every token: those are the "4 numbers" in the middle of the adapter. B is 4 by 768: four rows, four output directions. Whatever the input, the correction is always some mix of those same 4 rows of B. That is what "rank 4" means. Training learns what the 4 columns of A detect and which way the 4 rows of B push.

[169–172] Rank, and why fine-tuning edits are usually low rank
Rank, plainly: the number of different directions a matrix can push its outputs in. Look at each row of an edit matrix as one direction it can add to the output. Left grid: every row is a multiple of (1, 2, 0, 1): only one direction: rank 1. Middle: every row is a mix of two rows, (1, 2, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1, 0): rank 2. Right: four genuinely different rows: rank 4, the most a 4 by 4 matrix can have. A·B with r rows in B can never have rank above r, however big the matrix: that is LoRA's restriction, and its saving: 589,824 numbers become 6,144 for r = 4. Click: why is a rank-4 edit enough, when GPT-2's matrices could take a rank-768 edit? One: the skills are already there. Pretraining built the features; fine-tuning for one goal, more periods, politeness, a format, mostly turns a few existing features up or down. That is a few directions, not hundreds. Two: it has been measured. The LoRA paper fine-tuned GPT-3 and found that even r = 1 or 2 did about as well as full fine-tuning on their tasks. Three: Aghajanyan and colleagues (2020) showed fine-tuning has few real knobs: optimising just 200 numbers, mapped into the full model, reached 90% of full fine-tuning on a RoBERTa task. Four: it is a bet, not a law: a big new skill may need a larger r, which is why r is a hyperparameter.

[173] An adapter: keep W frozen, add a small trainable side path
The notebook's LoRA picture, redrawn sideways. A layer normally takes its input x, 768 numbers for one token, and multiplies by its pretrained weight W. Fine-tuning would change W itself. LoRA leaves W frozen and adds a side path next to it: an adapter. Click: the side path. The small box between A and B is the "4": x has 768 numbers; multiplying by A (768 by 4) gives just 4 numbers; multiplying those by B (4 by 768) gives 768 numbers again, the correction; then it is scaled by α/r. The 4 is the rank r: the whole correction has to pass through 4 numbers, so it can only push the output in 4 directions. Click: the two paths are added: the output is the original output plus a correction. Click: that is what an adapter is: the A → B side path attached next to a frozen weight. It never edits W; it learns a correction that is added to W's output. Only A and B are trained. What changes during training: only A and B. The notebook puts one of these on each attention matrix. The next slides: why squeezing to 4 numbers is enough, every symbol, and how it trains.

[174–176] LoRA, symbol by symbol
The formula from the notebook's Lora class: output = x W + (x A) B times α over r. Row by row. x: the input to this layer for one token, d_in numbers, 768. W: the pretrained weight, d_in by d_out; frozen. A: d_in by r, projects down to r numbers; trained; starts random (kaiming uniform in the notebook). B: r by d_out, projects back up to the output size; trained; starts at exactly 0. r, the rank: how many directions the correction can use; we choose it, 4 in the notebook. α, lora_alpha: a fixed number we choose, 32 in the notebook, that sets the overall size of the correction. α / r: the actual multiplier, 32 / 4 = 8. The notebook says it plainly: the larger the rank, the more we scale down the effect of the LoRA block. Why (xA)B and not x(AB)? AB would be a full 768 by 768 matrix: wasteful. Computing xA first keeps everything small. Which numbers are trained: only A and B.

[177] How LoRA trains: gradients reach only A and B
How it actually trains. The forward pass is the formula: both paths, added. The backward pass: the objective's gradient flows back through the network as usual, through both paths. But W is frozen, requires_grad is False: no gradient is stored for it and Adam keeps no averages for it. A and B do get gradients, and Adam updates only them. Click: step 0. B starts at zero, so the correction is zero and the model is exactly GPT-2: π_PPO equals π_base and the KL starts at 0. Click: why B = 0 but A random? With B = 0 the start is exactly GPT-2. With A random, x A is not zero, so B still gets a non-zero gradient at the very first step; A starts moving one step later, once B is non-zero. If both were zero, both gradients would be zero forever: stuck. If both were random, the model would not start as GPT-2. Click: after training you can add A B α / r into W once: one ordinary matrix, the same architecture as GPT-2, no extra cost when you use the model.

[178–181] Choosing r and α, and how many parameters that is [SKIP IF SHORT]
How are r and α chosen? They are hyperparameters: we pick them. Left: what r costs on GPT-2 small with the notebook's setup, adapters on W_Q, W_K, W_V and W_O of every head in all 12 layers. Per layer that is 4 matrices times 12 heads times r times (768 + 64) numbers. r = 1: about half a million. r = 4, the notebook: about 1.9 million, 1.5% of the model. r = 16: 7.7 million. r = 64: 31 million, a quarter of the model: at that point you lose most of the saving. Click: r is how much the adapter can learn: start small and raise it only if the task needs more. Click: α: keep it fixed; the notebook uses 32. Because the correction is scaled by α / r, the LoRA paper notes you can change r without retuning the learning rate much, so people pick α once and leave it. Click: where to put adapters: the original LoRA paper used the attention matrices, which the notebook copies; newer work also adds them to the MLPs, a bonus exercise in the notebook. Click: in section 2 the trainable set is the LoRA adapters plus the value head; everything else in GPT-2 is frozen.

[182] Attention LoRA: adapters on W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O
Where the notebook puts LoRA: the four attention matrices of every layer. Each head has its own W_Q, W_K, W_V of shape d_model × d_head, 768 × 64, and its own W_O, 64 × 768, so each head gets its own A and B; the Lora class handles all heads at once with n_inst. TransformerLens doesn't let us edit these matrices, so LoraHooks uses four hooks per layer, in order. Click 1: store the layer's normalised input, the input to Q, K and V. Click 2: when q, k and v are computed, add the adapter outputs. Click 3: store z, the input to W_O. Click 4: add the W_O adapter output, summed over heads, to the attention output; summing is fine because LoRA is linear. Click 5: Llama-style grouped-query attention has fewer key and value heads than query heads, so use n_kv_heads for k and v. Total on GPT-2 small, rank 4: about 1.9 million trainable numbers, 1.5% of the model.

[183] The reference model comes free: just switch the LoRA hooks off
The bonus that makes LoRA perfect for RLHF. The original weights W never change; only the adapters A and B learn. So with the LoRA hooks switched on, the model is the one we are training; with them off, it is exactly the original GPT-2: the frozen reference we need for the KL penalty. One copy of GPT-2 in memory instead of two: self.ref_model = self.model. TransformerWithValueHeadLora adds the hooks and the value head; only the LoRA parameters and the value head are trained.

[184] Part: GRPO
Notebook section 3. The actor and the objective stay; the critic is deleted and the advantage comes from a group of answers to the same prompt.

[185–186] GRPO: get "usual" from a group, not from a critic
Section 3. Why would we want to drop the critic? Left: what the critic costs in section 1. A value head and its loss to train, with its own learning rate and a warm-up. It starts random, so early advantages are noisy. And forecasting a single end-of-text reward from a half-written text is genuinely hard. Click: GRPO's idea, from DeepSeekMath, 2024. To know what is "usual" for a prompt, don't train a network to predict it: just sample several answers to that same prompt and take their average reward. Click: this closes the circle on "what is usual". VPG averaged over games. PPO learned a critic network. Section 1 used a value head on GPT-2. GRPO goes back to an average, and it is a fair average because every answer in the group had the same prompt. Click: the notebook's own sentence.

[187] GRPO deletes the critic; the actor trains as before
To answer "do we change the critic or the actor or both": GRPO deletes the critic and keeps the actor exactly as it was. Row by row. The actor, GPT-2 with LoRA, is still the policy and is still trained, with the same clipped objective. The critic, the value head, is deleted: no head, no critic loss, no compute_advantages. The advantage of a token is the only thing computed differently: in section 1 it came from the critic's forecast jumps; in GRPO it is the completion's reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation, the same number for every token of that completion. The rollout is the same 128 samples of "This is"; since they all share one prompt, the 128 ARE the group. The reference model is the same trick, LoRA hooks off. The objective loses one term: clipped term plus entropy minus KL. Click: the notebook's objective, with every symbol: ρ is the importance weight π_new over π_old, exactly as in PPO; Â_t is the normalised reward; ε is the clip range 0.2; β is the KL weight, our kl_coef; the 1/T averages over the T generated tokens.

[188–191] Scoring a group: one number per answer, copied to every token
A worked example with 4 completions of "This is" from the batch (illustrative texts, real arithmetic). Click: their rewards, the period counts: 3, 1, 2 and 6. Click: group mean 3, group std 2.16 (the notebook's normalize_reward uses torch's std). Subtract the mean: 0, −2, −1, +3. Click: divide by the std: 0, −0.93, −0.46, +1.39. That is each ANSWER's advantage. Click: and yes, every token of an answer gets that same number. Bottom row: completion 4 written out token by token: " it", ".", " Done", ".", and so on: every one of them gets +1.39, the periods and the words alike. In the clipped objective each token's importance ratio is multiplied by its advantage, so all of completion 4's tokens are pushed up by the same amount, and all of completion 2's tokens are pushed down by −0.93. Completion 1, exactly at the average, gets 0: no push at all. In the notebook the group is the whole batch of 128, because every sample starts from the same prompt; these advantages are literally normalize_reward(rewards), repeated over the 30 tokens.

[192] Per-token credit: the critic vs the group average [SKIP IF SHORT]
What do we give up? Compare the credit each method gives the tokens of our running example, " a list. Not much. Done.", reward 3. Click: section 1's critic: the periods get +0.8 each, " list" +0.1, " Not" −0.1, the rest 0: precise, per token. Click: GRPO: suppose this completion's group had mean 2 and std 1: then its advantage is (3 − 2) / 1 = +1.0, and every token gets +1.0, " Not" exactly as much as the periods. That is coarser. Why does it still work? Across thousands of completions, tokens that tend to appear in above-average answers, like periods, get pushed up more often than they get pushed down; tokens like " Not" appear in good and bad answers alike and roughly cancel out. It is the VPG trade again: noisier credit, but no critic to train. The notebook calls this outcome supervision, from section 4.1.2 of the GRPO paper: repeat the reward for every token.

[196] RLHF only shapes what you sample and score [SKIP IF SHORT]
From the notebook's reading (the shoggoth meme) and last cohort's session. RLHF only changes behaviour on the kinds of text it samples and scores: unusual prompts and jailbreaks can fall outside that. It optimises the number: in OpenAI's 2019 GPT-2 RLHF work, a sign bug made the model optimise for the worst-rated text while staying fluent, and nobody noticed until the run finished. And it narrows the model: less variety, favourite answers, mode collapse as we just saw.

What the KL penalty punishes

One position, after "This is a". Six possible next tokens. Dashed outline = the frozen original GPT-2 (ref_model). Filled = the model being trained. Drag the filled bars. Under each bar: original % → now %.

penalty = subtracted from the objective

1 · Two next-token distributions

At every generated position we compare the trained model's probabilities with the frozen original's. Right now they match: KL = 0.

2 · Each token adds a term

KL(new ‖ ref) = Σ new · log(new / ref). The small numbers under each bar are those terms. Drag any bar and watch which term grows.

3 · Invent something

Press Invent: "......" goes from 0.1% to 20%. Its term is 0.2 × log(200) ≈ 1.06: KL ≈ 0.88. Expensive: the original would almost never write that.

4 · Drop something

Press Drop: " story" goes from 20% to 0.1%. KL ≈ 0.22. Cheap: we may stop saying something the original said (useful for, e.g., offensive text).

5 · The reverse direction

Compare the second box. KL(ref ‖ new) swaps the costs: inventing ≈ 0.22, dropping ≈ 0.88. It would force the model to keep everything the original does, and tolerate new junk. Not what we want.

6 · Mode collapse costs little

Press Collapse onto " list": putting 90% on a token the original already liked is fairly cheap. That is how a KL-penalised model can still collapse onto a favourite.

7 · In the notebook

This is done at every generated position over all ~50k tokens: .sum(-1) over the vocab, .mean() over batch and positions, × kl_coef = 2.5, then subtracted.
